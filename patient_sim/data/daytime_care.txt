DAYTIME CARE
Activity, Recovery and Safety
Period: 08:00 — 22:00
Patient: Oleg I. T., 83 years | Post-stroke | Positive trajectory
SECTION 1. SAFETY: ATTEMPTS TO STAND
How to respond to attempts to stand
Do not leave him unsupervised while awake and active — especially if he reaches to get up.
Calmly explain: 'We'll stand up together soon, with the doctor. It's too early now.' He understands speech — explanation works.
Raising the head of the bed (30-45°) partly satisfies the wish to be upright, safely.
If bed rails are available — raise them. Keep the nurse-call button within reach of his healthy left hand.
First real verticalization — only with doctor/physical-therapist approval, in stages: first sitting with legs down, then standing with support.
SECTION 2. DAYTIME POSITIONING
2.1 Sitting position (main during the day)
Head of bed raised to 30-60° while awake — aids breathing, swallowing, communication and digestion.
Back straight, not sliding sideways; pillows at the sides for symmetry.
Right arm NOT dangling — on a pillow or bedside table in front of him, hand open.
Still change position every 2 hours (shift support even when sitting) to avoid sacral pressure sores.
After meals keep elevated another 30-40 minutes (aspiration prevention).
2.2 Arm and leg positioning (when lying between activity)
Same as the night guide:
Right arm: pillow under the whole arm, palm up, fingers open, no clenching into a fist.
Right leg: heel free, foot at 90°, roll against outward rotation.
Do not place on the right (affected) side.
SECTION 3. DAYTIME ACTIVITY (gentle, measured)
3.1 Limb movement
Right arm: passive and active movements (flexion-extension), mirror therapy — watch the healthy left arm move. Since he makes a fist, encourage any movement attempts.
Right leg: active movements (nearly recovered) — knee flexion, lifting, as tolerated.
Propping on elbows is a good sign; encourage in a controlled way, but without attempts to stand.
3.2 Sitting with legs down
When the doctor/physical therapist allows — sit him with legs over the edge of the bed for a few minutes, supported and supervised. This trains balance before standing.
Watch for dizziness, pallor, BP drop — return to lying if they appear.
3.3 Breathing exercises
Given pneumosclerosis and bronchitis — deep breathing, gentle exhalations several times a day (prevents congestive pneumonia).
SECTION 4. SPEECH AND COGNITIVE STIMULATION
4.1 How to communicate during the day
Talk more — ask questions he can answer briefly or by gesture. Each answer is practice.
Praise speech efforts, do not correct harshly. Give time to find the word.
English words — support them (you may sometimes address him with familiar English words), but Russian is the recovery base.
Simple tasks: name an object, count, repeat a short phrase — gently, as a game, not an exam.
If a speech therapist comes — that is the day's main speech work; family supplements, not replaces it.
4.2 Emotional support
Do not say 'don't cry' — let him feel the emotion, support gently.
Redirect to the positive when appropriate: photos, familiar music, news from loved ones.
If crying turns into uncontrollable episodes (involuntary crying/laughing) — note it and tell the doctor: post-stroke pseudobulbar affect is treatable.
SECTION 5. SLIGHT FACIAL ASYMMETRY
Slight right-sided facial asymmetry, barely noticeable — expected with a left-hemisphere stroke (which controls the right side of the face). Against the overall improvement this is not alarming, but part of the same picture as the arm paresis.
Observe: whether the asymmetry worsens (worsening — tell the doctor).
Watch the corner of the mouth during drinking/eating — risk of leaking, choking. Feed from the healthy side.
Facial exercises — per the speech therapist's advice (puff cheeks, smile, wrinkle the forehead).
SECTION 6. DAYTIME WARNING SIGNS (call the on-call doctor)
On weekends — an on-call doctor. These signs require calling immediately, not waiting until Monday:
Worsening facial asymmetry, new weakness, speech worse than in the morning.
Worsening consciousness, confusion, excessive drowsiness.
Sharp BP rise >160 or a drop; rhythm disturbances.
Choking, coughing when eating/drinking, noisy breathing — aspiration risk.
A fall or an attempt to stand ending in a bump — examination mandatory (the implant!).
Bleeding (nose, gums) on anticoagulation; temperature >38°C.
Uncontrollable episodes of crying/laughing (pseudobulbar affect) — not urgent, but note it.
What to record for the treating physician by Monday
Speech trajectory: which new words, how much better he answers.
Motor activity: what is new (propping on elbows, attempts to sit).
BP, HR, temperature, oxygen-saturation figures with times.
Emotional state, crying episodes, sleep.
Appetite, swallowing, whether there was a bowel movement.
