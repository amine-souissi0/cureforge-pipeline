4.2 The family's role — training the communication partner
Communication Partner Training has a strong evidence base (Simmons-Mackie et al., 2016). Family members learn to: (1) acknowledge the patient's competence, (2) give time and tools to respond, (3) use gestures, pictures, and writing.
4.3 How to communicate correctly in motor aphasia
IMPORTANT: he understands speech (sensory aphasia is gone) but cannot speak voluntarily. This is frustrating for him.
HELPS: short closed questions ('Want water?'), picture cards, a calm tone, time to respond
HELPS: speak normally, not as to a child — he is an adult and understands everything
DOES NOT HELP: long explanations, demands to 'say it properly', constant re-questioning
NORMAL: if he mixes languages — this is a compensatory strategy, a sign of recovery at work, not deterioration

5. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS
5.1 Swahili — a low-resource language for therapy
There are practically no controlled studies of aphasia therapy in Swahili. Bilingual speech therapists with knowledge of Swahili are almost impossible to find in Russia. Practical conclusion: formal therapy in Swahili is likely unavailable — the language should be maintained through contact with family/community and recorded materials (songs, prayers, family conversations in Swahili on audio).

5.3 Does multilingualism help or hinder?
The evidence (Alladi et al., 2016; Lahiri et al., 2021) indicates: multilingualism does NOT hinder recovery and probably helps — through enhanced cognitive control and 'cognitive reserve'. Fears that 'dividing attention across three languages' would be harmful are not scientifically supported.
6. ACTION PLAN
Urgent (next 4-8 weeks)
Formal assessment by a bilingual speech therapist using the BAT (Bilingual Aphasia Test) — at minimum the Russian and English modules, ideally Swahili via telemedicine. This gives a baseline across all three languages.
Begin intensive speech therapy in Russian >=3 hours per week, combining: Melodic Intonation Therapy, Semantic Feature Analysis, Script Training on 5-10 meaningful phrases.
Start training the main Russian-speaking family member in the communication-partner role.
Maintain daily contact with English and Swahili: 15-30 minutes each day — conversation, news, music, video calls. Do NOT cram — the goal is emotional engagement and exposure.
Install Constant Therapy or Lingraphica on a tablet for independent practice (target >=30 minutes a day).
Weeks 8-24 (subacute period — the most productive)
Add cross-language training once Russian plateaus: the same concept in Russian, then English, then Swahili in one session (training translation equivalents). This maximizes transfer.
Repeat the BAT assessment every 8-12 weeks — for early detection of divergence in language recovery.
Long-term (6 months and beyond)
Transition to a maintenance regimen: 1-2 sessions with a speech therapist per week + daily app practice + family conversation. Recovery continues for years.
Group conversational therapy in Russian (if available locally).
What should change the plan
No improvement on BAT after 6-8 weeks of Russian therapy -> increase intensity, change method, check for depression/hearing/new lesions
English or Swahili begins to WORSEN (rare antagonistic recovery) -> add short maintenance sessions in that language
Pathological language-mixing becomes noticeable and disruptive -> add language-control training
IMPORTANT CAVEATS
Most data on bilingual aphasia come from small studies and case reports. The meta-analyses by Goral (2023) and Lee & Faroqi-Shah (2024) are the most rigorous, but pool heterogeneous data.
The 'Russian + English + Swahili' combination is essentially unstudied. Recommendations are extrapolated from a broader base (mainly Spanish-English, English-Hebrew, Indian languages, and the single Russian-English case of Kiran & Iakupova).
The 'bilingual cognitive reserve' is disputed — the stroke data are observational, not replicated in Western populations. Treat as probable, not proven.
Ribot's and Pitres' 'laws' are simplifications. The modern model accounts for many factors: age of acquisition, pre-stroke proficiency, language of the environment, emotional significance.
The recovery picture may change over time — the profile at 1 month will not necessarily match the profile at 6 months. Reassess regularly.
